IQ Score Chart: What Is a Good, Average or High IQ?

By the IQScore Fun team

You've got a number. Maybe from a test, maybe from our quiz. Now you want to know what it means. Is 108 good? Is 124 impressive? Is 96 something to worry about?

Here's the chart most people are looking for, followed by the context that makes it useful.

The IQ score chart

Most modern IQ tests are built so that the average score is 100, with a standard deviation of 15. Labels for the ranges vary a little between tests and publishers, so treat the names below as a general guide, not an official classification.

IQ rangeCommon descriptionApprox. share of people
130 and aboveVery high / gifted rangeabout 2%
120 - 129High / superiorabout 7%
110 - 119Above averageabout 16%
90 - 109Averageabout 50%
80 - 89Below averageabout 16%
70 - 79Borderline / lowabout 7%
Below 70Significantly below averageabout 2%

Those percentages are approximate, and they add up to roughly 100 because they come from the bell curve that test makers design the scores to follow.

So what counts as a "good" IQ?

Honestly, anything from about 90 to 110 is "average," and that's where half of all people land. If you're in that band, you're exactly where most of the population is. There's nothing wrong with it. The people you admire most are probably spread across the whole chart.

A score above 110 is above average. Once you reach 130 or higher, you're in roughly the top two percent. Societies like Mensa set their entry bar at about that level on an approved, supervised test.

A lower score, say in the 80s, doesn't predict what a person can achieve in life. It tells you how they did on a particular set of tasks, and, if it comes from a professional assessment, it can be a useful clue about where someone might benefit from support. It's not a ceiling.

Why the extremes are shaky

You'll sometimes see claims of IQs of 160, 180 or even higher. Be skeptical. Standard tests are designed to measure the broad middle of the population accurately. At the extreme ends, there simply aren't enough people in the reference sample to make the numbers meaningful, and the margin of error gets large. Very high numbers you see in headlines are usually estimates, extrapolations, or from non-standard tests.

Margin of error: your score is a range, not a point

Even on professional tests, a score isn't exact. Psychologists usually report a confidence interval, such as "somewhere between 105 and 115." If you take a similar test twice, you won't get identical results, and a few points either way is completely normal. So don't read much into the difference between 118 and 122. Both are in the same neighborhood.

Myths worth dropping

  • "A high IQ guarantees success." It helps with some things, but persistence, opportunity, health and relationships matter a lot too.
  • "Online tests that say you're a genius are accurate." If a test gives most people a score above 130, it's flattering you.
  • "IQ is the same thing as knowledge." You can learn facts at any age. IQ tests try to look at reasoning, not trivia.
  • "My IQ is my destiny." It isn't. It's one number from one test on one day.
