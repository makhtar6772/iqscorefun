What Is IQ? A Simple Guide to Intelligence Scores

By the IQScore Fun team

IQ is one of those terms everybody uses and few people can explain. It gets thrown around in arguments, shows up in movie plots and gets stamped on online quizzes. So let's slow down and go through what it actually is, in plain language.

The basic definition

IQ stands for intelligence quotient. It's a number that shows how your performance on a set of reasoning tasks compares with other people in your age group. The word "quotient" is a leftover from early tests, which literally divided a child's "mental age" by their real age. Modern tests don't do that anymore (we explain why in our piece on average IQ by age), but the name stuck.

The idea of testing reasoning goes back to a French psychologist, Alfred Binet, who in 1905 was trying to help schools spot children who needed extra support. If you're curious about how it grew from there, we've written a short history of IQ tests.

How the scale works

Most major tests are designed so that the average score is 100 and the standard deviation is 15. If "standard deviation" makes your eyes glaze over, here's what it means in practice. About two out of three people score between 85 and 115. About 95 out of 100 score between 70 and 130. And only around two in a hundred score above 130 or below 70.

That bell shape is deliberate. Test makers adjust the scoring so that the results of a large, representative group of people fall in that pattern. A score of 100 doesn't mean "you got 100 points." It means "you're right in the middle of the pack." You can see what the different ranges are usually called in our IQ score chart.

What IQ tests actually measure

The tasks on a typical test fall into a few families:

  • Reasoning and logic: working out what follows from a set of rules.
  • Pattern recognition: spotting the rule behind a sequence of numbers, letters or shapes.
  • Verbal ability: understanding words and the relationships between them.
  • Working memory: holding information in your head while you use it.
  • Processing speed: how quickly you complete simple mental tasks.

Psychologists have found that people who do well in one of these areas often do well in others. That shared thread is sometimes called general intelligence, or g. It's the reason a single score can be useful at all. It's also why the score doesn't cover everything: there's a lot of human skill that never shows up in these tasks.

What IQ tests don't measure

IQ says nothing direct about creativity, kindness, curiosity, discipline, emotional skill, or practical know-how. Plenty of people with a modest score build excellent careers and relationships, and plenty of people with a high score struggle with motivation or with other people. If you want a fuller picture, read about the different types of intelligence.

A single score is also a snapshot. It can shift with sleep, stress, mood, health and how familiar you are with the style of puzzle. Professional testers know this and report scores with a margin of error. A person who gets 112 one day could reasonably get 107 or 117 another day, and that isn't a sign anything is wrong.

Is IQ fixed?

Not exactly. Scores in adulthood are fairly stable compared with childhood, but they aren't frozen. Education, health, sleep, and even getting used to a type of puzzle can change how you perform. Over a lifetime, the abilities underneath the score change in different ways, too: raw problem-solving speed tends to dip slowly with age, while vocabulary and general knowledge can keep growing.

What you can't do is "hack" your way to a different intelligence in a week. What you can do is improve your performance on a certain kind of test by practicing it. Our guide on preparing for an IQ test shows how.

Where IQ is actually used

In the real world, formal IQ testing is mostly handled by psychologists. It's used to help identify learning difficulties, to assess educational needs, and in some clinical and research settings. It's not generally used to rank ordinary people against each other, and responsible professionals are careful not to treat a number as a verdict on someone's potential.

Online tests: fun, but not official

Quick online tests, including ours, can't match this. They haven't been normed on thousands of people, they're short, and nobody supervises the conditions. They're good for fun and practice. If you need an official result for any serious reason, you'll need a qualified professional. We go into this in online IQ tests versus real ones.
