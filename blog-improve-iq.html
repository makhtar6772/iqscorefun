10 Science-Backed Ways to Sharpen Your Thinking

By the IQScore Fun team

Let's get one thing straight before the list. No habit is going to turn you into a different person overnight, and anyone who promises "+20 IQ points in a week" is selling something. What you can do is look after the machine your thinking runs on, and practice the skills these puzzles use. Both make a real, noticeable difference in how sharp you feel and how well you perform.

Here are ten things with reasonable evidence behind them, in roughly the order I'd start.

1. Sleep properly

This is the boring one that beats nearly everything else. Sleep is when your brain sorts what you learned during the day, and a short night hits attention, memory and problem-solving quickly. If you're doing a timed puzzle test on five hours of sleep, you're competing with one hand tied behind your back. Aim for seven to nine hours, and keep wake-up times fairly regular.

2. Move your body

Regular aerobic exercise, like brisk walking, cycling or swimming, is linked in many studies with better memory and attention and with healthier aging of the brain. You don't need a gym membership. Thirty minutes of walking most days is a perfectly good start.

3. Learn something that's actually hard

A new language, a musical instrument, coding, chess, a craft: the point is that it stretches you and keeps stretching you. Things that are easy don't build much. Pick something challenging enough that you feel a bit clumsy at first, and keep going until the clumsiness fades.

4. Practice the puzzle types themselves

This is the most direct way to do better on tests like ours. Number sequences, letter series, analogies and matrices all become faster once the common tricks feel familiar. We lay these out step by step with examples in our guide to preparing for an IQ test. Keep in mind that this improves your performance on this style of puzzle, which isn't the same as becoming generally more intelligent, but it's a real and useful improvement.

5. Read, and read widely

Reading builds vocabulary and background knowledge, and both of those help you understand new information quickly. Mix it up: fiction, history, science, long articles that take effort. If you only ever read short posts, you're training for short posts.

6. Eat reasonably

There's no miracle brain food, and anyone selling one is overselling. But overall dietary patterns that are rich in vegetables, fruit, whole grains, fish and nuts, and lower in heavily processed food, are generally associated with better long-term brain health. Staying hydrated and not skipping meals before a test helps too.

7. Stay socially connected

Conversation is a surprisingly good mental workout. You're following someone's argument, remembering context, reading tone and forming a reply, all at once. Isolation, on the other hand, tends to go along with worse mental health and lower mental activity. Call a friend, join a club, argue (politely) about something.

8. Manage stress

Long-running stress makes it harder to concentrate and to remember things. That doesn't mean you need to be calm all the time. It means building in recovery: breaks, time outdoors, hobbies that have nothing to do with work, and enough downtime that you're not constantly on alert. A few minutes of slow breathing before a test is cheap and surprisingly effective.

9. Teach what you learn

Try explaining an idea to someone else, or even to an imaginary student. You'll quickly find the gaps in your own understanding. It's one of the oldest and most reliable study techniques there is, and it works for logic and math problems as much as for history.

10. Review your mistakes

After any test or puzzle, don't just look at the score. Go through the questions you got wrong and ask why the right answer is right. That's where most of the learning happens. It's why we include an explanation for every question in the answer review after our test.

A word on "brain training" apps

Large research reviews have generally found that practicing a brain game makes you better at that game and sometimes at very similar tasks, but the benefits don't reliably spread to everyday thinking. That doesn't make puzzles pointless. They can be enjoyable, and they're good practice for puzzle tests. Just don't expect a few weeks on an app to change how smart you are in general. Pair them with the lifestyle basics above, and you'll get a much better return.

Where to start

If you only do two things, make them sleep and exercise. They're free, they work, and they make every other item on this list easier. Once those are in place, add a challenging hobby and some regular puzzle practice.
