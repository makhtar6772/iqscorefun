A Short History of IQ Tests: From Binet to Today

By the IQScore Fun team

IQ tests have a surprisingly modest origin story. They didn't begin as a way to rank everyone's brainpower. They began as a practical tool for a school problem in Paris. How they got from there to online quizzes and dinner-party arguments is a mix of useful science, big ideas and some deeply uncomfortable chapters.

Paris, 1905: a test to help children

In the early 1900s, France had made school compulsory, and teachers suddenly faced classrooms full of children with very different needs. The government asked a psychologist named Alfred Binet to find a fair way to identify the kids who needed extra help, so they wouldn't just be written off as lazy or disobedient.

Together with his colleague Théodore Simon, Binet created a set of short tasks that got steadily harder: following instructions, naming objects, repeating numbers, comparing things. Children were scored by how they did compared with others their age. The 1905 Binet-Simon scale is generally treated as the first practical intelligence test.

Binet himself was cautious. He didn't think his test measured a fixed, inborn quantity, and he worried it would be misused to label children permanently. That worry turned out to be well founded.

Where the term "IQ" came from

The idea of expressing the result as a single ratio is credited to the German psychologist William Stern, who in 1912 suggested dividing mental age by chronological age. Multiply by 100 to remove the decimal point, and you get the "intelligence quotient." We explain why that formula was later replaced in our article on average IQ by age.

America takes it up

At Stanford University, Lewis Terman revised Binet's test for American use, publishing the Stanford-Binet scale in 1916. It was widely used, and it's the version that made "IQ" a household word in the United States.

Then came World War I. The U.S. Army needed to sort huge numbers of recruits quickly, so psychologists developed group tests, the Army Alpha for those who could read and the Army Beta for those who couldn't. It was the first time millions of people were tested, and it showed that mass testing was possible. It also set the stage for a lot of public confidence in tests that was, frankly, ahead of the science.

The uncomfortable chapter

This is the part of the story that doesn't get told enough. In the early twentieth century, some prominent supporters of IQ testing used the scores to argue for ideas that we now recognize as discriminatory and unscientific. Test results were used to support restrictive immigration policies and eugenics programs in the United States and elsewhere. Tests were written in a way that favored people from certain cultural and educational backgrounds, and differences that came from schooling, language and poverty were presented as differences in inborn ability.

That history is a big reason why modern psychologists are careful about what they claim, why test publishers put effort into reducing cultural bias, and why many people remain wary of IQ talk. It's a good reminder that a test is only as fair as the way it's built and used.

Wechsler changes the format

In 1939, psychologist David Wechsler published a new test, the Wechsler-Bellevue scale. He wasn't satisfied with the Stanford-Binet, which leaned heavily on language and was designed for children. His scale included both verbal and performance tasks, and it introduced the deviation IQ, comparing a person with others of the same age, with 100 as the average.

Later versions of his tests became the standard in the field. The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) and the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC) are still widely used by psychologists today, in updated editions.

Pattern tests and "culture-fair" attempts

In 1936, John Raven developed Raven's Progressive Matrices, a test made almost entirely of visual patterns with a missing piece. The goal was to reduce the influence of language and schooling. You may have seen the same style on our own test, in the 3x3 grids with a question mark. No test is perfectly free of cultural influence, and pattern tests still depend on familiarity with the format, but they became an important tool for comparing people from different backgrounds.

The Flynn effect and modern debates

From the 1980s onward, the researcher James Flynn showed that raw scores had been rising for decades in many countries. This forced test makers to re-norm their tests regularly, and it started a lasting argument about what the rise means: better nutrition and health, more schooling, more exposure to abstract thinking, or something else. There's no single agreed answer.

Today, serious IQ testing is done one-on-one by trained professionals, with long, standardized batteries that measure several abilities at once. They're used mainly for educational placement, clinical assessment and research, not for ranking people at parties.

Then came the internet

Online IQ tests arrived in the 1990s and 2000s and spread quickly, because they're cheap, quick and people love seeing a number. Most of them have nothing like the statistical grounding of the professional tests. That doesn't make them useless, but it does mean you should treat them as entertainment and practice, which is exactly how we describe ours. For more on this difference, read online IQ tests versus real ones.

What to take from all this

The history of IQ testing shows a tool with real uses and real limits. It started as a way to help children who were falling behind. It was then stretched, and sometimes badly abused, far beyond what it could support. The best modern practice is more modest: measure a specific set of skills carefully, explain the limits clearly, and don't confuse a score with a person's worth.
